## First Job, Five People, No Safety Net

<ArticleDiagram visualId="polymagine-industry-4-eyewear-2017" />

Fresh out of engineering school, I signed my first permanent contract at **Polymagine**. There were five of us. So there was not much of a ladder to climb: mostly problems to solve, and not many other people around to solve them.

It was 2017, well before the "metaverse" wore everyone out and before 3D face scanning became a mundane smartphone feature. The project: **Industry 4.0 applied to eyewear**. Scan a face, generate a frame fitted to that exact face, let the customer try it on in augmented reality, then manufacture it.

## The Stack: Java, C++, and a Library Whose Name I Forgot

Scanning happened on iPads, but also on a specialized tablet. Behind it, the geometric core ran on Java and C++, with a computational geometry library whose name I have completely forgotten. I gave it a significant share of my twenties. Such ingratitude.

On the infrastructure side, the architecture was distributed on Kubernetes, on GCP, with instances dedicated to generating the 3D meshes. Geometry computation does not like sharing its machine with anything else.

Real-time rendering went through three.js, embedded in a React app. And for the pretty pictures, we set up a Blender machine dedicated to PBR rendering: it turned every model into a virtual shop window, with reflections on the acetate the real frame could only dream of.

Fun fact: for those renders, we had access to the most powerful graphics cards of the time (Titans, if memory serves). We rented them as spot instances for a few minutes, just long enough for a render. Pure luxury, billed by the minute.

## One Second, Because I Said So

The one-second target did not come from any spec. I set it myself, then pushed every performance knob as far as it would go, with one rule: no compromise on quality.

It was all the more demanding because, after I arrived, we also reworked the generation algorithms. The glasses we proposed became much more realistic. Faster and prettier at the same time is the kind of challenge you mostly accept when you have just left school.

## The Hall of Horrors

Procedural generation means spectacular failures. I saw glasses with missing parts. Very avant-garde, not very wearable.

The early versions of head tracking brought their share of glitches too. When face tracking drops out, the frame quickly wanders off to live its own life. It was quite something to see.

I still have a soft spot for those runaway glasses.

## From Scan to Manufacturing

The augmented reality demo was the visible part. The project aimed further: the same data flowing from the biometric scan all the way to production, with the digital design feeding directly into manufacturing the frame. That is what made it a real Industry 4.0 project, rather than a fancy photo filter.

## What Happened Next

Polymagine did not manage to sell the product. The technology worked; the go-to-market, much less so.

In 2024, walking into a store, I came across a virtual try-on screen. It offered more or less what we had built seven years earlier. I smiled, a bit like running into an ex who has done very well for themselves.

In a startup, being right too early looks a lot like being wrong. But I know where my habit of pushing performance knobs all the way comes from.
