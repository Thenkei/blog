## Context

A central data team quickly becomes a queue: every business question requires a ticket, an extract, and another explanation. Self-service analytics promises autonomy. At RockFi, that autonomy must preserve stricter constraints: confidentiality, auditability, and consistent definitions for assets, flows, clients, and performance.

## The problem

Making the warehouse queryable does not make its numbers trustworthy. Two syntactically correct queries can disagree on grain, effective date, contract status, or cancellation treatment. The disagreement often appears only after a number reached a dashboard or informed a decision.

The problem is not access to data. It is access to **verifiable semantics**.

## Bad ideas

- Open every table and call it self-service.
- Certify a dashboard without certifying the metrics it combines.
- Duplicate domain logic in each query “to move faster”.
- Present an AI-generated answer without provenance, freshness, or confidence.
- Govern every exploration like regulatory reporting and eliminate exploration entirely.

These approaches confuse freedom to explore with authority to publish.

## What did not work

Data dictionaries alone aged too quickly. They described columns but rarely captured exclusions, expected grain, or the point when a metric becomes final.

“Source of truth” dashboards did not work when they embedded private SQL. They moved the contract into a visualisation tool, made it hard to test, and created multiple truths as soon as another dashboard reimplemented the calculation.

Letting an agent query raw sources directly also produced fast but fragile answers: it selected a plausible join without knowing which domain invariant the join had to preserve.

## Alternatives

Three levels should remain separate:

1. **Exploration**: bounded access, temporary output, visible assumptions.
2. **Certified metric**: versioned definition, owner, tests, and freshness policy.
3. **Decision or publication**: displayed provenance, access controls, and review proportional to impact.

A semantic layer can carry shared dimensions, grains, and formulae. A catalogue can expose ownership and freshness. Governed marts can isolate sensitive data. None of those tools replaces the domain contract.

## The solution

We define every shared metric as a product with a minimum contract:

| Element | Question to settle |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning | Which decision does this number support? |
| Grain | What does one row represent? |
| Population | Which entities are included or excluded? |
| Time | Event date, effective date, or snapshot? |
| Owner | Who resolves ambiguity? |
| Evidence | Which tests and reconciliations protect the calculation? |
| Freshness | When is the result no longer usable? |

AI assistants receive certified contracts and models first. They may propose an exploration, but must expose sources and flag when a question exceeds the certified boundary. Output remains reviewable before publication.

## Limits

This approach has a cost: contracts must be maintained, definition changes handled, and freshness monitored. Not every metric deserves that governance. A one-off analysis can remain explicitly exploratory.

A semantic layer cannot repair a wrong source or a missing event. It can even standardise a bad definition. Trust comes from a chain of evidence and accountability, not a “certified” badge.
