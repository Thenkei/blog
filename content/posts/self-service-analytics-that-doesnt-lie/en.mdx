## In the beginning, everyone had their own Metabase

At RockFi, self-service analytics started in the most natural way possible: a Metabase plugged into the data, and everyone writing their own queries. At the time, nobody thought about agreeing on strong metric definitions. You had a question, you wrote some SQL, you had an answer. No ticket, no waiting, no committee.

If you have ever opened a Metabase instance older than six months, you know what comes next: the “Assets” question sits next to “Assets (copy)”, which sits next to “Assets v2 DO NOT USE”, which someone obviously uses every Monday. Every copy starts from good intentions and drifts one `WHERE` clause at a time.

## Plenty of wrong numbers, all easy to unmask

This regime produced plenty of inconsistent numbers, and a few fine blunders. The typical scene: two people walk into an internal meeting with two values for the number of clients, not even in the same ballpark, and the agenda gives way to a comparative reading of `JOIN` clauses.

Both queries were often “correct”. One counted clients, the other accounts. One used the transaction date, the other the value date. One excluded cancelled rows, the other cheerfully added them up. No SQL engine fails over that.

```sql
-- Identikit of a Metabase question from that era (names made up)
SELECT COUNT(*)                 -- clients? accounts? yes.
FROM accounts a
JOIN positions p ON p.account_id = a.id  -- the join that multiplies in silence
WHERE p.status != 'cancelled'   -- copied from another question, never reread
  AND p.date >= {{start_date}}  -- which date? whichever one was in the table
```

To be fair about one thing: every time, we found the source of the problem easily. The query was right there in Metabase, two clicks away. We opened it, spotted the missing filter or the duplicating join, and fixed it. The chaos was unpleasant, but traceable. That is also why we took our time before structuring anything: as long as an error fits in a query you can read, it costs a meeting, not a project.

## Ten or so partners, as many ways to correct a mistake

What kept the chaos manageable was that all the logic lived in the query. That holds up less well when part of the logic lives in the source data itself.

Our data comes from about ten external partners, with different transmission methods. And when a partner corrects a record, it does not necessarily do it like its neighbour. Some post reversals: the original row stays, an offsetting row cancels it, and a new row carries the right value. Others rewrite history: the past row changes in place, and yesterday's file no longer quite matches the day before's.

Writing a correct query under those conditions means knowing how each partner behaves. In the “everyone has their own Metabase” world, that knowledge is re-encoded in every query, by every author, at whatever level of knowledge they had that day. The query stays readable; what it assumes about the sources does not.

## Snowflake, dbt, Dagster, and Cube, which did not stay

The current stack, built after that era, looks like this:

- **Snowflake** as the warehouse;
- **dbt** for modelling and the semantic layer: that is where metric definitions are written, versioned, and tested, once, instead of being copied into every question;
- **Dagster** as the orchestrator, chosen before this work began.

We also evaluated **Cube** as a dedicated semantic layer. It did not meet our needs, so we set it aside.

The whole thing is still evolving: what follows describes work in progress, not a finished reference architecture. What has changed is where the discussion happens: in a shared definition that anyone can reread and challenge, rather than between two screenshots in a meeting.

## A metric's passport

Before a metric feeds a shared dashboard, it has to answer a few questions. Nothing exotic, but each one could tell the story of its own derailed meeting:

| Element | Question to settle |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning | Which decision is this number meant to support? |
| Grain | Is one row a client, an account, or a position? |
| Population | Who is in: closed accounts, clients still onboarding, cancelled rows? |
| Time | Event date, effective date, or snapshot? |
| Owner | Who decides when two readings are both defensible? |
| Evidence | Which tests and reconciliations against the sources protect the calculation? |
| Freshness | When does the result stop reflecting the partners' latest deliveries? |

The “Time” row looks like the most academic one. It is the trickiest, and our partners make sure to prove it.

Take the assets for a given month, and a correction received the following month. With a partner that posts reversals, the correction arrives as new rows dated on the day of the correction: last month does not move, and the current month absorbs the difference. With a partner that rewrites history, it is last month that changes, retroactively, without anyone touching the dashboard.

Depending on the date you choose, “the month's assets” do not have the same value:

- on a raw **event date**, you mix both behaviours: last month is corrected for partners that rewrite, not for those that post reversals;
- on an **effective date**, you attach each correction to the transaction it corrects: everyone converges, but every reversal has to be linked to its original row, and last month's number moves after the fact;
- on a **snapshot**, you freeze what you knew at month end: the number presented last month stays last month's number, provided you kept the deliveries as they were, since some partners have rewritten them since.

The last two readings are both defensible; they simply answer different questions. What is wrong is not saying which one you chose, or letting the first one settle in by default because `date` was the column in the table. Hence the order of operations: the model must first normalise the two ways of correcting, the metric then chooses its date, and the passport writes it down in black and white.

## Explore, certify, publish

The goal was never to kill self-service. An exploratory question does not need a passport: it needs bounded access to the models, temporary output, and visible assumptions. That is the spirit of the Metabase era, which we wanted to keep, on firmer ground.

A shared metric, on the other hand, has a versioned definition, an owner, tests, and a freshness policy. And a number used to make a decision, or one that leaves the team, displays its provenance, respects access controls, and goes through a review proportionate to what it commits us to.

## An agent plugged into raw tables

The current temptation is to plug an AI assistant straight into the warehouse and ask it the question. On raw tables, it would replay the Metabase era at machine speed: a plausible join, a date picked from whatever columns were there, a reversal counted twice. With one extra flaw: if nobody sees the query it improvised, we lose the only virtue of our early chaos, which was being able to find the source of the error easily.

So our assistants start from certified models, show the query and the sources they used, and flag when they leave that boundary. Before publication, someone must be able to reread the result the way we used to reread a Metabase question.

## What the semantic layer does not fix

A flawless definition does not fix a wrong partner file: it cleanly computes a wrong number. Reconciliations against the sources remain essential, and they do not write themselves.

Contracts also need maintenance. A partner that changes how it corrects its data silently invalidates an assumption in the model, and the “certified” badge keeps shining in the meantime. It is only worth the tests and the owner behind it.

And not everything deserves this treatment. A one-off analysis can stay exploratory, and that is fine. An “Assets (copy)” question can keep existing; it has just lost the right to present itself as the reference.
