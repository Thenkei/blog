## Three dashboards walk into a meeting

Nothing tests confidence in data quite like three dashboards answering the same question with three different numbers. Every query runs. Every chart looks respectable. Each result may even be internally consistent. The meeting still has to stop and ask a more basic question: what, exactly, did we count?

This is why self-service analytics is attractive. A central data team quickly becomes a queue, where every business question requires a ticket, an extract, and another round of explanation. People need room to investigate without waiting at that counter.

At RockFi, however, autonomy has to preserve confidentiality, auditability, and consistent definitions for assets, flows, clients, and performance. A fast answer is not much help if nobody can retrace how it was produced.

## SQL can be right while the number is wrong

Making a warehouse queryable does not make its numbers trustworthy. Two syntactically correct queries can disagree on grain, effective date, contract status, or cancellation treatment. The difference may remain invisible until the number reaches a dashboard or influences a decision.

The real product is therefore not access to data. It is access to **verifiable semantics**: enough shared meaning to understand, reproduce, and challenge a result.

## The warehouse is not a vending machine

Opening every table is easy to present as self-service: choose a dataset, press a button, collect a number. It also pushes all the difficult domain decisions onto the person writing the query.

Certifying a dashboard is not enough if the metrics inside it have no contract. Copying domain logic into each query “to move faster” works until two copies evolve differently. An AI answer without provenance, freshness, or confidence only hides the same problem behind a friendlier interface.

The opposite extreme is just as unhelpful. If every rough exploration is governed like regulatory reporting, people stop exploring. Freedom to investigate should not be confused with authority to publish.

## Where the “source of truth” starts multiplying

Data dictionaries help, but they age quickly when they describe columns and omit exclusions, expected grain, or the moment a metric becomes final.

A “source of truth” dashboard can be equally fragile when its definition lives in private SQL. The domain contract has merely moved into a visualisation tool, where it is hard to test. As soon as another dashboard reimplements the calculation, one source of truth becomes two.

Letting an agent query raw sources directly is the faster version of that mistake. The agent can select a plausible join without knowing which domain invariant the join must preserve. The result arrives quickly; the uncertainty arrives later.

## Give every shared metric a passport

We treat a shared metric as a small product. Before it travels into dashboards, analyses, or decisions, it needs a minimum contract:

| Element | Question to settle |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning | Which decision does this number support? |
| Grain | What does one row represent? |
| Population | Which entities are included or excluded? |
| Time | Event date, effective date, or snapshot? |
| Owner | Who resolves ambiguity? |
| Evidence | Which tests and reconciliations protect the calculation? |
| Freshness | When is the result no longer usable? |

The contract does not make the metric permanently correct. It makes disagreements inspectable: there is a definition to compare, an owner to ask, and evidence to rerun.

## Self-service needs three lanes

Not every question should pass through the same controls. We keep three levels distinct:

1. **Exploration** allows bounded access, temporary output, and visible assumptions.
2. **Certified metrics** add a versioned definition, owner, tests, and freshness policy.
3. **Decisions or publications** display provenance, enforce access controls, and receive review proportional to their impact.

A semantic layer can carry shared dimensions, grains, and formulae. A catalogue can expose ownership and freshness. Governed marts can isolate sensitive data. These are useful pieces of infrastructure, but none replaces the domain contract.

## Make the assistant show its working

AI assistants receive certified contracts and models first. They may propose an exploration, but they must expose their sources and flag when a question crosses the certified boundary. Before publication, the output remains something a person can review—not an answer promoted to fact because it appeared in a polished sentence.

## A badge is not evidence

This model creates maintenance work. Contracts evolve, definition changes need handling, and freshness needs monitoring. Not every metric deserves that investment; a one-off analysis can remain explicitly exploratory.

Nor can a semantic layer repair a wrong source or recreate a missing event. It can standardise a bad definition just as efficiently as a good one. Trust comes from the whole chain of evidence and accountability, not from a “certified” badge at the end of it.
