At the start of 2026, the AI conversation had become a race with too many finish lines. Every week brought a new model, a new benchmark result, a new interface, or a new claim that one laboratory had finally taken the lead.

I kept hearing the same question: **which LLM is the best?**

After following the products and using them for engineering work, I think the question is already too small. We are not comparing only chatbots. We are comparing research organisations, model families, agent runtimes, editors, clouds, distribution channels, and the cost of turning intelligence into a dependable service.

This is my January 14 snapshot of that race. It is deliberately a snapshot: later releases do not belong in the evidence for what the frontier looked like at the beginning of the year.

## A frontier is not one leaderboard

“Frontier model” sounds like a single category, but the frontier moves along several axes at once.

One model may be strongest at long-context reasoning. Another may be better at multimodal understanding. A third may solve coding tasks with fewer tokens, run more cheaply, or fit inside an organisation that cannot send its data to a hosted API. A fourth may be less impressive in a benchmark but already be available in the product where millions of people work.

The useful comparison therefore needs at least seven dimensions:

- reasoning quality and reliability;
- coding and tool use over long tasks;
- context length and information handling;
- latency, token efficiency, and inference cost;
- multimodal capability;
- openness, customisation, and deployment control;
- distribution, integrations, and operational governance.

The best score on one axis does not settle the others. A model can be technically ahead and commercially behind. A model can be open and inexpensive while requiring more engineering to operate. A product can make an external model feel more useful through better context selection, tools, and interaction design.

That last point matters because the names we use are not all at the same level.

This is why the old comparison between “Claude, Gemini, Grok, and Cursor” can become misleading. Claude, Gemini, and Grok name model families and the products built around them. Cursor is primarily a development environment that can select and orchestrate models. Codex and Claude Code sit between the two: they are products whose value depends on both a model and an agent runtime.

The race is not only to train the smartest model. It is to own the most valuable layer around it.

## OpenAI and Anthropic: intelligence became a working surface

OpenAI entered January with GPT-5.2 and GPT-5.2-Codex released in December. The important shift was not only another increase in benchmark scores. The releases framed the model as infrastructure for professional work, long-context reasoning, tool use, and project-scale coding. Codex made the same argument from the engineering side: the frontier was becoming useful when it could sustain a task, not merely answer a prompt.

Anthropic was making a similar move through Claude and Claude Code. The model was part of the story, but so were the surrounding habits: repository exploration, careful edits, tool calls, review, and the ability to keep a long task coherent. Anthropic’s January announcement of Labs made that direction explicit. Claude was no longer just a conversational destination; it was becoming a family of agentic surfaces.

I do not read this as proof that one company had solved reliable autonomy. It shows that the competitive unit had changed. A frontier model that cannot be directed, inspected, stopped, or integrated into a real workflow is an impressive component, not a finished capability.

## Google and xAI: distribution is part of intelligence

Google’s Gemini 3 arrived before the new year with an unusually strong combination of multimodal reasoning, agentic development, and access through Google’s existing products. Google’s structural advantage is not simply that it trains models. It can connect research, specialised hardware, Cloud, Search, Android, and developer tools into one distribution system.

That makes the question “is Gemini the best model?” less useful than “where can Gemini become the default reasoning layer?” A model available inside the tools people already use has a different kind of power from an isolated API, even if the underlying model comparison is close.

xAI represented another strategy. Grok was attached to a real-time social platform, while xAI was raising the capital required to build a much larger compute base. The bet was not only on model quality. It was on a tight loop between live information, a large existing audience, and enough infrastructure to keep training and serving the model.

These strategies are difficult to compare with a benchmark table because distribution changes the feedback loop. Usage produces data, integrations create habit, habit creates more usage, and infrastructure determines how much of that loop a company can afford.

## Meta, DeepSeek, Mistral, and Qwen: the open frontier

The closed-model race attracted most of the attention, but the open-weight frontier changed the terms of competition.

Meta’s Llama strategy treated the model as an ecosystem substrate. Developers could customise it, run it in different environments, and build tools around it without accepting the same degree of vendor dependence as a hosted model. That does not automatically make an open model better. It makes control, deployment, and experimentation first-class parts of the value proposition.

DeepSeek demonstrated why inference economics belongs in the definition of the frontier. Its V3.2 release combined reasoning and tool-use claims with an explicit focus on open models and efficiency. The significant challenge to the larger laboratories was not simply “a cheaper model.” It was the possibility that architectural and post-training choices could reduce the amount of compute required to reach a useful level of capability.

Mistral’s December release placed a similar emphasis on deployable open models, including Mistral Large 3 and smaller models. Its position was not just a European branding exercise. Hosting location, licensing, customisation, and control over sensitive workloads can be decisive for companies that cannot treat model access as a neutral SaaS purchase.

Qwen showed another route: a broad open family with different sizes and hybrid reasoning modes. The existence of many model sizes matters operationally. A single flagship model may win a benchmark, but the model family that can move from a server to an edge device to a specialised workflow may win more deployments.

Open weights therefore create a different definition of winning. The winner may not be the model that answers one difficult question best. It may be the model that becomes the most useful component in thousands of systems its original laboratory does not control.

## Cursor and the application layer

Cursor belongs in this story, but not as another entry in a foundation-model leaderboard.

Its January work on dynamic context discovery and agent best practices made the distinction visible. Cursor’s agent harness supplies instructions, tools, context selection, and workflow conventions around the models it supports. That surrounding system can materially change what a model accomplishes in an editor.

This is the layer where many users experience the frontier. They do not call a raw model and inspect its isolated output. They open a repository, ask for an investigation, accept or reject edits, run tests, and decide whether the result deserves a pull request. The quality of that loop depends on the model, but also on retrieval, permissions, terminal access, latency, diff presentation, checkpointing, and review ergonomics.

The same logic applies to Codex and Claude Code. A coding model is only one part of an agent product. The runtime defines what the model can see and do; the interface defines how a person can direct it; the workflow defines how its output becomes a trusted change.

The application layer may therefore capture more value than the model layer. It owns the relationship with the user, the accumulated context, the task history, and the point where intelligence meets a real decision.

## What the benchmarks miss

Benchmarks are useful signals, but they are weak substitutes for a working evaluation.

First, many results are reported by the laboratories that built the models. The task selection, prompting, tool configuration, reasoning budget, and scoring choices all matter. A score without its evaluation setup is not a portable fact.

Second, different benchmarks reward different behaviours. A model that performs well on a static academic test may be less useful when it must inspect an unfamiliar repository, recover from a failed tool call, preserve an invariant, or explain uncertainty.

Third, the model changes faster than our mental rankings. A name like “Claude” or “Gemini” hides a moving family of snapshots, modes, and product integrations. A comparison can become stale while still sounding precise.

For engineering work, I would measure time to reliable evidence rather than tokens generated. Can the system find the right context? Can it make a bounded change? Can it show what it did? Can a human review the result without reconstructing the entire session? Can the organisation afford the latency, data path, and supervision cost?

These questions do not make benchmarks irrelevant. They put them in their proper place: evidence about one capability under one setup, not a universal ranking of intelligence.

## The January answer

So, which LLM was the best at the start of 2026?

There was no honest single answer.

OpenAI and Anthropic were pushing model capability toward long-running professional and coding work. Google had the strongest full-stack distribution story. xAI was combining a live platform with aggressive infrastructure ambitions. Meta, DeepSeek, Mistral, and Qwen were proving that openness, efficiency, customisation, and deployment control could define a frontier of their own. Cursor showed that the best way to use a model could matter as much as the model’s isolated ability.

The race had not produced one winner. It had produced several competing definitions of progress.

That is the conclusion I find more useful as an engineer. I do not need to defend a permanent champion. I need to choose the system whose capabilities, controls, cost, and failure modes fit the work in front of me. The winning model is often the one that lets a team reach a reliable decision with the least total friction.

## Limits

This article is a historical snapshot as of January 14, 2026. It deliberately excludes later launches, revised model families, and new product positioning.

The comparison also relies partly on company-published claims. Those sources are valuable for understanding what each organisation was trying to build, but they are not independent proof that every claim generalises to every workflow. The frontier is moving too quickly for a static table to remain authoritative for long.
