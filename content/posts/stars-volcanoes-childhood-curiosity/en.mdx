For as long as I can remember, two directions have drawn me in: far above us and deep beneath our feet.

On one side are stars, constellations, and every other celestial body. On the other are volcanoes. They may look like unrelated fascinations. To me, they come from the same place: a desire to understand immense phenomena through what we can observe.

## Looking up

I have watched the stars since I was a child. I look for constellations, try to recognise their shapes, and can easily become absorbed by everything in the night sky.

I never needed this curiosity to be useful. Looking was enough. The sky makes an overwhelming scale immediately perceptible: distances that are difficult to conceive, objects whose light has travelled for staggering amounts of time, and more questions than immediate answers.

That fascination never really left me. It probably explains why the Rocket theme feels so much like me. It represents more than a spacecraft or a space backdrop. It gives room to the part of me that still keeps looking up.

## Finding constellations

What I like about constellations is the passage from observation to understanding. At first, there are only points of light. Then you learn to distinguish them, name them, and find your bearings.

I suspect part of my taste for engineering comes from the same process: look at something complex, identify a few stable points, then try to understand the relationships between them.

Of course, I did not look at the sky in those terms as a child. I looked because it drew me in. The rest came later.

## Looking beneath our feet

On Earth, the phenomenon that has always fascinated me most lies almost in the opposite direction: volcanoes.

They expose forces that usually work outside our view. They reshape landscapes, remind us that the planet is always changing, and create a peculiar combination of beauty and unease.

Perhaps that is why I was good at biology and Earth science at school. Or perhaps I was good at those subjects because they already fascinated me. I no longer know which way the relationship developed.

## The same curiosity

Stars and volcanoes ultimately say the same thing about me. I am drawn to systems that cannot be grasped in a single glance, to the traces they leave, and to the work required to reconstruct how they operate.

Astronomy makes me look beyond Earth. Volcanoes remind me how complex, active, and precious this Earth is in its own right.

I have never considered those directions contradictory. Looking at the sky does not have to mean wanting to leave our planet. It can also help us better understand the place we occupy here.

## A small hidden passage

That is what I like about this logbook: the Rocket theme becomes more than an appearance. It opens a passage to more personal writing about the subjects that attracted me before I knew what to do with them.

Stars and volcanoes were there long before my first program. The curiosity they created is still here.
