I increasingly use AI to draft RFCs, explore an approach, or work through a refactoring. It changes how I spend my time: less writing the first version, more reviewing it and deciding what to keep.

Understanding constraints and making decisions were already a large part of staff engineering. With AI, I can examine more proposals. I still need to understand why one fits and where the others would cause problems.

## Comparing approaches

<ArticleDiagram visualId="ai-force-multiplier" />

When I explore an event-driven architecture, I can ask for several approaches and compare their implications: where the transaction boundary falls, how retries work, and what happens when a consumer falls behind.

The proposals give me a starting point. To choose, I have to return to the system's constraints and check the assumptions behind each approach. An example that works in isolation may leave out delivery guarantees or the constraints of an existing service.

## What I check before keeping the code

I want to be able to explain the change, understand its side effects, and know what validates it. Asking the model to justify a choice can expose an assumption; its answer cannot confirm that assumption on its own. I go back to the relevant code, contracts, and tests.

If I do not understand a line, I investigate why it is there before changing the code. Deleting it simply because I cannot explain it could remove necessary behaviour.

The same applies to a bug the agent appears to have fixed. I need to connect the symptom to its cause and verify that the change addresses that cause. Without that check, I cannot tell whether the problem was fixed or moved elsewhere.
