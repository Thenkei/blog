When people talk about AI agents, they often ask how far the system can act on its own. Working on an on-duty workflow led me to a different question: what does an agent pass on when it stops?

An agent that investigates, then leaves behind a pile of logs and an unexplained decision, makes the next person start over. It is the software equivalent of a colleague leaving for holidays with a sticky note that says “it’s the cache”. The hand-off matters as much as the investigation.

## It started with on-duty chores

On duty, you need to find useful data, understand what changed, check the repository and runbook, then decide what to do. Context is scattered across dashboards, tickets, and earlier investigations, and the clock is running.

The questions barely change from one alert to the next. Is this a real incident or a noisy signal? Which system is actually responsible? What changed recently? What evidence would separate the likely causes? And is the safest next step a rollback, a patch, a configuration change, or simply watching a little longer?

The first steps repeat too: compare current and expected state, look for recent changes, run known diagnostics. Those were the chores we handed to small, specialized agents.

## Small agents, close to the work

One agent collects evidence, another knows the relevant codebase, and a third tests a hypothesis or prepares a patch. The person on duty spends less time gathering context and more time interpreting the results.

> We did not automate responsibility. We automated investigation.

In practice, the person on duty still decides: which agent to call, in what order, and when there is enough evidence. Agents inspect, compare, and propose, and their output alone does not authorise a risky change.

## What happens when the agents call one another?

Once those agents exist, it is tempting to chain them: collect data, inspect the repository, prepare a patch, validate it. But the human in the middle was doing more than pressing “next”.

They weighed evidence, noticed contradictions, dropped disproven hypotheses, and decided when another opinion was needed. If agents call one another, that orchestration has to become explicit.

An orchestrator may continue a well-supported, low-risk task or ask for a review. To choose, it needs the investigation state rather than the last answer: the objective, the paths already ruled out, what has been tried, the evidence behind the current hypothesis and what is still missing, how confident we are, and what the next actions risk.

Without that, the chain keeps moving and loses the thread.

## 14:03, and then what?

Retrieving the right documents and granting the right tools gets you halfway. The other half is passing on the reasoning built from them.

Here is a hand-off that looks helpful:

```text
The error rate increased at 14:03.
```

True, precise, and nearly useless on its own: whoever reads it has to redo the investigation to learn what it means. Here is one the next person can actually use:

```text
Investigation: elevated error rate in the checkout service

Current assessment: likely regression in the payment retry path
Confidence: medium

Evidence:
- Errors began shortly after the latest checkout deployment.
- The increase is concentrated in retry-related requests.
- The dependent payment provider does not show a corresponding increase.

Ruled out:
- A broad provider outage
- A database saturation event

Not yet checked:
- Whether the new retry configuration is active in every region

Recommended next actions:
1. Compare the active configuration across regions.
2. If the configuration is inconsistent, pause rollout and prepare a rollback.
3. Escalate before changing production configuration.
```

The hypothesis, the evidence, the discarded paths, and the remaining uncertainty are all there. The next agent, or the human, can pick it up without starting from zero.

## A place to take over

An event could trigger an automatic investigation, after which the orchestrator posts a structured hand-off in a Slack thread. The person on duty would see the evidence, hypotheses, confidence, and possible actions at the moment their judgment is needed.

The same format could live in a ticket, pull request, or incident timeline. Between agents, too, a compact state beats a raw transcript that each recipient must reinterpret.

## Designing a good hand-off protocol

A hand-off should make six things explicit:

1. **The original objective**, so the chain does not drift.
2. **The current hypothesis** and confidence level.
3. **The evidence and its provenance**, so others can check it.
4. **The paths ruled out**, so they are not repeated.
5. **The next step**: investigate, review, approve, or escalate.
6. **The cost of being wrong**, which determines whether a person must decide.

Confidence alone does not settle it. A low-risk diagnostic can run automatically, while a production change may need review even when the hypothesis looks strong.

Asking for approval at every tiny step brings the bottleneck back, and skipping checkpoints altogether makes mistakes expensive. The middle ground is to let the system run bounded diagnostics, then pause before an ambiguous or high-impact action. At that point the person gets enough context to challenge the reasoning, choose an option, and add what the agents could not know.

## Hand-offs are part of reliability

The reliability of an agent workflow also depends on what the next participant understands. Can they tell observation from hypothesis, check the evidence, and know whether a recommendation is ready to execute or only worth investigating?

So evaluation should look at the hand-off, and not only at each agent's answer. An unfinished investigation still helps if it narrows the possibilities and says clearly what remains uncertain.

Our on-duty workflow taught us that an agent needs to know when to proceed and when to ask for judgment, and that it must keep the thread of the investigation as the work changes hands. The test I now apply is short: when the agent stops, does the next person know what to check and which decision to make?
