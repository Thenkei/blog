When people talk about AI agents, they often ask how far the system can act on its own. Working on our on-duty workflow led me to a different question: what does an agent pass on when it stops?

An agent that investigates, then leaves behind a pile of logs and an unexplained decision, makes the next person start over. The handoff matters as much as the investigation.

## It started during on-duty

On duty, you need to find useful data, understand what changed, check the repository and runbook, then decide what to do. Context is scattered across dashboards, tickets, and earlier investigations, and you do not have much time.

The questions barely change from one alert to the next. Is this a real incident or a noisy signal? Which system is actually responsible? What changed recently? What evidence would separate the likely causes? And is the safest next step a rollback, a patch, a configuration change, or simply watching a little longer?

The first steps repeat too: compare current and expected state, look for recent changes, run known diagnostics. Those were the chores we handed to small, specialized agents.

## An agent for each step of the investigation

One agent collects evidence, another knows the relevant codebase, and a third tests a hypothesis or prepares a patch. The person on duty spends less time gathering context and more time interpreting the results.

We automated the investigation. The decision stays with the person on duty: which agent to call, in what order, and when there is enough evidence. Agents inspect, compare, and propose. Their output alone does not authorise a risky change.

## What happens when the agents call one another?

Once those agents exist, it is tempting to chain them: collect data, inspect the repository, prepare a patch, validate it. But the human in the middle was doing more than pressing “next”.

They assessed the evidence, noticed contradictions, dropped disproven hypotheses, and decided when another opinion was needed. If agents call one another, that orchestration has to become explicit.

An orchestrator may continue a well-supported, low-risk task or ask for a review. To choose, it needs the state of the investigation, and the last answer is not enough: the objective, the paths already ruled out, what has been tried, the evidence behind the current hypothesis and what is still missing, how confident we are, and what the next actions risk.

Without that, the chain keeps moving and loses the thread.

## A handoff that says too little

Retrieving the right documents and granting the right tools is not enough. The reasoning built from them has to be passed on too.

Take a real alert, anonymised here. This handoff looks helpful:

```text
The error rate increased at 14:03.
```

It is accurate and precise, and almost useless on its own: whoever reads it has to redo the investigation to learn what it means. Here is what you can pass on so that they can pick it up:

```text
Investigation: elevated error rate in the checkout service

Current assessment: likely regression in the payment retry path
Confidence: medium

Evidence:
- Errors began shortly after the latest checkout deployment.
- The increase is concentrated in retry-related requests.
- The dependent payment provider does not show a corresponding increase.

Ruled out:
- A broad provider outage
- A database saturation event

Not yet checked:
- Whether the new retry configuration is active in every region

Recommended next actions:
1. Compare the active configuration across regions.
2. If the configuration is inconsistent, pause rollout and prepare a rollback.
3. Escalate before changing production configuration.
```

The hypothesis, the evidence, the discarded paths, and the remaining uncertainty are all there. The next agent, or the human, can pick it up without starting from zero.

## A place to take over

This is in use on duty today: the orchestrator posts a structured handoff in a Slack thread. The person on duty finds the evidence, the hypotheses, the confidence, and the possible actions there when they have to decide.

The same format could live in a ticket, pull request, or incident timeline. Between agents, too, a compact state is better than a raw transcript that each recipient has to reinterpret.

## What a handoff should contain

A handoff should make six things explicit:

1. **The original objective**, so the chain does not drift.
2. **The current hypothesis** and confidence level.
3. **The evidence and its provenance**, so others can check it.
4. **The paths ruled out**, so they are not repeated.
5. **The next step**: investigate, review, approve, or escalate.
6. **The cost of being wrong**, which determines whether a person must decide.

Confidence does not decide everything. A low-risk diagnostic step can be started automatically, while a production change may need review even when the hypothesis looks solid.

Asking for approval at every tiny step brings the bottleneck back, and removing every checkpoint makes mistakes expensive. What we aim for is in between: the system runs bounded diagnostics on its own, then stops before an ambiguous or high-impact action. At that point the person gets enough context to challenge the reasoning, choose an option, and add what the agents could not know.

## Evaluate the handoff too

The reliability of an agent workflow also depends on what the next participant understands. Can they tell an observation from a hypothesis, check the evidence, and know whether a recommendation should be executed or only looked into?

So evaluation should look at the handoff, and not only at each agent's answer. An unfinished investigation still helps if it narrows the possibilities and says clearly what remains uncertain.

Our on-duty workflow taught us that an agent needs to know when to proceed and when to ask a human, and that it must keep the thread of the investigation when the work changes hands. The test I now apply fits in one sentence: when the agent stops, does the next person know what to check and which decision to make?
