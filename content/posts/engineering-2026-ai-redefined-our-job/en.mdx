New year, new paradigm. Not the HR-approved "new year, new me" kind that evaporates by February. Funny timing, though: February is roughly when the way we work flipped, and this one doesn't look like it's going to evaporate.

At **RockFi**, we crossed that line without really planning to. Looking back, I might as well say it plainly: writing code is no longer our main activity. We spend far less time fighting syntax, and far more deciding what to build and checking that what got built actually holds up.

## February: Leaving Cursor for Claude Code

<ArticleDiagram visualId="engineering-2026-ai-redefined-our-job" />

Around February, the tech team (10 to 12 people at the time) moved from Cursor to **Claude Code**. You could read it as yet another shiny tool to help us forget our technical debt. What we were actually betting on was different: an AI that works in the terminal and the repo, in the middle of the messy reality of the project, instead of finishing our lines of code.

Nobody had to mandate the migration. People saw the difference between wrestling with context limits and actually finishing their tasks, and they switched on their own.

We don't type any faster. What we gained is **leverage**: Claude Code understands our spaghetti architecture well enough to absorb the soul-draining translation between "what I want" and "what the compiler forces me to type."

> We write far less code by hand than we used to. And yet we ship more. Said out loud, it still sounds like heresy.

## Homegrown Tools That Do the Grunt Work

One of the best calls we made was building our own **internal tools** on top of Claude Code. Nothing cosmetic. They are narrow, opinionated tools, each aimed at one specific source of friction, the kind that makes you want to flip your desk:

- **Workshop and brainstorming support**: structured prompts and context loaders so we can explore ideas without falling down a rabbit hole of obsolete Jira tickets.
- **Dive In generator**: our deep technical documentation tool. That kind of doc used to eat half a senior engineer's day and was outdated the day it was published. Now it's generated alongside the code. `// No more docs lying to production.`
- **Story-to-PR pipelines**: we write the user story, the tool does the rest. It creates the branch, pulls the context, drafts the description and links the artifacts. The administrative tax we used to pay on every feature is gone.

None of these tools make decisions for us. They remove the mindless friction that slowly burns out good engineers, and that's already a lot.

## Codex and Claude Code (aka Tooling Polygamy, Owned)

On top of the team's shared stack, I ended up with a more pragmatic personal setup: I run **Codex** and **Claude Code** side by side, and I have no intention of picking one.

Codex is my scalpel for pure code generation. Tightly focused, great for short loops where you just need the damn function written. Claude Code brings the heavy artillery on context: it's what I open for the back-and-forth of an architecture decision, or to debug the gnarly distributed issue that only shows up at 3 AM.

With that pair, I work across **4 to 5 projects a day** without my brain melting. An observability regression to fix in one repo, a terrifying schema migration to review in another, and my main project moving forward in between. The context switching that used to crush my spirit before lunch has become bearable.

I didn't become a multitasker, my brain is still single-threaded. The tool just keeps track of each repo, so picking a topic back up two hours later costs me almost nothing.

## Beyond "AI Makes Us Faster"

Boiling all this down to "we code faster" misses what actually moved:

- **The unit of effort shifted.** We used to judge progress by how many lines we pushed to `main`. Now we judge it by problems solved, architecture decisions we can defend, and how the system holds up under load. `// Your AWS bill will finally appreciate it.`
- **Juniors learn different things.** Boilerplate and syntax, which used to fill a good part of their early days, go to the LLM. What's left for them to learn is intent, the business domain, and the fine art of not creating circular dependencies.
- **We finally do actual engineering.** Reading complex systems. Making painful tradeoffs. Designing for failure. Reviewing code to make sure it won't bring the database connection pool to its knees. `// Don't set concurrency to 100 unless you truly hate your database.` The hard stuff is now the core of the job.

## The New Bottlenecks (aka Ways This Will Inevitably Explode)

Writing code costs almost nothing now, but physics still applies: the bottleneck just moved.

- **Code review paralysis (and AI reviewers):** reading code is harder than writing it. Reviewing an AI's PR is like reviewing a "10x" engineer who never sleeps, knows everything, and occasionally hallucinates a cryptographic library that doesn't exist. Reading 500 generated lines while hunting for the race condition quietly slipped into the middle is a muscle we didn't have. Our only viable defense is fighting fire with fire: dedicated reviewer skills in Claude (and their counterparts in Codex) now do the first pass. `// Let the bots argue about cyclomatic complexity before I have to look at it.`
- **Context is the new syntax:** we stopped fighting syntax and started fighting context windows. Drop an LLM into a 5-year-old monolith without guidance and it will confidently rewrite your payment logic following an outdated architectural paradigm it read on Reddit in 2021. Knowing how to "talk to AI" isn't enough anymore: you have to carefully pick the context you feed it. We've become _context engineers_.
- **The API dependency:** time for some self-deprecation. The day the Anthropic or OpenAI API goes down, the whole team grinds to a halt. We'll stare blankly at our IDEs, because manually typing out a 150-line boilerplate schema migration will feel like being asked to churn our own butter. The dependency is total, and it's a risk we took with our eyes open.

## Fast Typing Doesn't Hide Anything Anymore

Let's say it out loud: **what counts as an engineer's contribution has changed for good.** Typing 120 WPM was never the actual job, but it was a great place to hide a lack of critical thinking. That hiding place is gone.

If you aren't working on your system judgment, your ability to evaluate AI output without mercy, and your instinct for telling _what is solid_ from _what vaguely compiles_, 2026 is going to chew you up. The more the tools produce, the more you need someone able to say no to what they produce.

## Back to Work

Eight months ago, I wouldn't have bet on a team shipping more, without breaking production, while writing less code by hand. Yet here we are, and the line between "designing" and "writing" code has become genuinely blurry.

What's left is raising the level of the humans as fast as the level of the tools. And keeping someone on the team who can still write a migration by hand, for the day the API goes down.
