AI performs a very old magic trick: it makes the machinery disappear.

I open a chat window, type a question, and an answer appears. Nothing on screen shows the mines, factories, or power lines behind it.

I work in software and see what AI makes possible: comparing architectures or prototyping an idea in minutes. It changes which ideas are worth trying and makes judgment and verification more important. I believe in that transformation. My hesitation begins beneath the interface.

## An inconvenient confession: I believe the pitch

Two recent essays by Dario Amodei and Mark Zuckerberg show the promise the industry sees.

Amodei takes the concentration of power, economic disruption, and model risks seriously. He still argues that these risks can be managed and that this transition can improve the world. [The Adolescence of Technology](https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology)

Zuckerberg argues for widely available personal intelligence rather than superintelligence controlled by a few companies or governments. [The Future Is for Everyone](https://about.fb.com/br/news/2026/07/o-futuro-da-ia-e-para-todos/)

I share their concern about concentrated power. AI can also open up projects in creation, research, and learning that were previously out of reach. But this story often leaves out its physical foundation.

“The cloud” hides data centres, processors, cooling, power lines, and supply chains. Follow that chain and you reach minerals, factories, workers, and territories.

A service can be easy to access and unequal in its production. People do not share the same connectivity, ability to pay, or influence over its infrastructure. The front door may be open to everyone; the building still belongs to someone and stands somewhere.

## The power socket has joined the roadmap

The International Energy Agency estimates that data-centre electricity consumption grew by 17% in 2025, with consumption from AI-focused data centres growing faster still. It projects global data-centre electricity use rising from roughly 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030, while also pointing to pressure on grids, transformers, turbines, chips, and supply chains. [IEA, Key Questions on Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

Those figures do not measure AI alone. They show the pace of growth. Electricity must be generated and delivered in specific places, where local grids may not be ready. Data centres can quickly create heavy demand, along with investments local communities do not always control.

AI may help optimise grids, but that future benefit does not erase the cost of building infrastructure today.

## When “less per task” becomes more in total

The IEA reports that hardware and software can reduce energy use per task. Yet usage is rising, and video generation or agents require more than a simple text query. Each use can become cheaper while total consumption grows.

Water use varies with location, cooling, climate, and electricity source. There is no universal number for “water used by one query”. The United Nations Environment Programme also notes how difficult it is to measure AI's full footprint. [UNEP, Environmental Impact of AI](https://www.unep.org/technical-highlight/how-make-ai-data-centres-more-sustainable)

Amodei sees water concerns as insignificant. That may hold in some contexts; generalising across an industry with such local resource demands seems premature to me.

## Access is the front door, not the whole house

Beyond “is AI good or bad?”, we need to ask: beneficial for whom, at what cost, using whose resources, and under whose control?

I believe AI can transform science, education, and creation. Democratising it still takes more than access to a tool: it also involves infrastructure choices, resource allocation, and the distribution of gains.

This revolution rearranges resources, dependencies, and power. How much of the world are we willing to mobilise for it, and who gets to decide?
