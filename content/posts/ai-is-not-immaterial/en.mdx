AI performs a very old magic trick: it makes the machinery disappear.

I open a chat window, type a question, and an answer arrives. There is no visible turbine, cooling loop, mine, factory, or power line. The interface feels almost weightless.

I work in software, so I also feel the other kind of magic. Work that once took hours can now be explored in minutes. I can compare architectures, prototype an idea, search documentation, or surface competing hypotheses with an ease that still surprises me.

That is not merely the same work at a higher speed. It changes which ideas are cheap enough to try. It moves more of the value toward framing the problem, exercising judgment, checking the result, and understanding what a decision will set in motion.

So yes, I believe we are living through a revolution.

My hesitation begins one floor below the interface.

## An inconvenient confession: I believe the pitch

The recent essays by Dario Amodei and Mark Zuckerberg interested me because they come from inside the transformation, not from spectators watching it pass.

Amodei does not offer a naive celebration of artificial intelligence. He takes seriously the concentration of power, economic disruption, military use, unpredictable model behaviour, and the indirect consequences of accelerating science. He nevertheless argues that those risks can be managed, and that a substantially better world may emerge from this difficult transition. [The Adolescence of Technology](https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology)

Zuckerberg approaches the problem through distribution. A superintelligence held by a handful of companies or governments would, in his view, be dangerous. His answer is broadly available personal intelligence: tools that let people create, learn, build businesses, and pursue their own goals. [The Future Is for Everyone](https://about.fb.com/br/news/2026/07/o-futuro-da-ia-e-para-todos/)

I am with them up to a point.

Concentrating extraordinary capabilities in very few hands is a political problem, not just a product-design choice. No single superintelligence could neutrally decide what a good life means for everyone. Societies differ, values collide, and pluralism has to remain more than a setting in an application.

I also resist describing AI only as a machine for replacing workers. It can become infrastructure for creation, research, and learning. It can make projects possible for people who previously needed a full team or resources they did not have.

Then the story floats upward into the cloud, and something important falls out of the frame: matter.

“The cloud” is a useful metaphor and a terrible inventory.

Artificial intelligence depends on data centres, processors, memory, networks, cooling systems, buildings, batteries, power lines, and international supply chains. Follow those far enough and you reach minerals, factories, workers, and territories.

The chat window hides this industrial chain beautifully. It does not make the chain disappear.

That is why “give everyone access” cannot settle the question of justice on its own. A resource may be widely accessible where it is consumed and profoundly unequal where it is produced. People do not share the same connectivity, electricity, hardware, ability to pay, or influence over the infrastructure behind the service.

The front door may be open to everyone. The building still belongs to someone, runs on something, and stands somewhere.

And the planet is not infinite.

## The power socket has joined the roadmap

This part is less cinematic than superintelligence, but it is difficult to route around.

The International Energy Agency estimates that data-centre electricity consumption grew by 17% in 2025, with consumption from AI-focused data centres growing faster still. It projects global data-centre electricity use rising from roughly 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030, while also pointing to pressure on grids, transformers, turbines, chips, and supply chains. [IEA, Key Questions on Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

Those figures are not an AI-only meter reading. They are a warning about the direction and the pace: industrial demand is expanding faster than the infrastructure expected to absorb it.

The issue is not simply whether the planet can produce enough electricity in the abstract. Electricity has to be generated, connected, and delivered in particular places. Local grids, generation capacity, permitting processes, and manufacturing supply chains are not automatically sized for the announced growth. Data centres cluster in specific regions and can bring large, sometimes variable power requirements on short timelines. New plants and transmission infrastructure may follow, along with investments that local communities do not always control.

AI may help forecast demand, optimise grids, and reduce certain forms of waste. I hope it does. But future optimisation does not cancel the present cost of building the system that will perform it.

## When “less per task” becomes more in total

Models and hardware are becoming more efficient. That is real progress, and it matters.

The IEA reports that hardware and software improvements can sharply reduce energy use per task. A simple text query may use relatively little electricity. Yet usage is rising, models are growing, and video generation, complex reasoning, and autonomous agents require far more than simple text generation.

Both statements can therefore be true: each use becomes cheaper while total consumption grows. Technology has seen this trick before. Lower the cost of doing something and people find many more occasions to do it.

Water makes neat comparisons even harder. The impact changes with location, cooling design, climate, and the electricity source. There is no honest universal number for “the water used by one query”. The United Nations Environment Programme emphasises both this variation and the difficulty of measuring AI's full lifecycle footprint. [UNEP, Environmental Impact of AI](https://www.unep.org/technical-highlight/how-make-ai-data-centres-more-sustainable)

That does not validate every alarming estimate. It does mean that imperfect measurement is not proof of an absent problem.

In his essay, Amodei dismisses concern about AI water use as insignificant. That may hold within certain boundaries, regions, or scenarios. Applied to a rapidly expanding industry whose infrastructure and resources are intensely local, it feels like too quick a conclusion.

## Access is the front door, not the whole house

I do not want to reject artificial intelligence. I also do not want the people building it to define every acceptable trajectory by default.

The familiar duel between catastrophe and enthusiasm is too small for the subject. “Is AI good or bad?” is the opening question, not the serious one. Beneficial for whom? At what cost? Using whose resources? Under which conditions? Controlled by whom?

A technology does not become just because everyone can log in. Infrastructure does not become sustainable because it sits behind glass and pixels. A promise of progress is not yet a balance sheet.

I can believe AI will transform science, education, engineering, and creation while insisting that we count what the transformation consumes. I can value distributed intelligence while noticing that models, chips, networks, and data centres remain controlled by a very small number of actors.

Real democratisation must extend beyond access to the tool. It has to reach decisions about infrastructure, resource allocation, acceptable uses, and the distribution of gains.

AI will not remain inside computing. It will shape energy, industry, research, medicine, education, defence, work, and probably the way society organises itself. That is exactly why treating it as one more software product is no longer enough.

Perhaps this revolution will rival electricity or personal computing in scale. Revolutions, however, do more than open possibilities. They rearrange resources, dependencies, and power.

The question is not only how far AI can go.

It is how much of the world we are willing to mobilise to get there — and who gets to decide what that expenditure means.
