I work in software, and I genuinely believe artificial intelligence will transform much more than computing.

I can already see it in the way I work. Tasks that once required several hours can now be explored in a few minutes. I can compare several architectures, prototype an idea, search through documentation, or surface competing hypotheses with a new level of ease.

This is not only an acceleration of execution. It changes what we can try. It lowers the entry cost for certain intellectual activities. It shifts value toward problem framing, judgment, verification, and the ability to understand the consequences of a decision.

In that sense, I do believe we are living through a revolution.

But a revolution is never defined only by what can be done from an interface. It also depends on everything that must be built behind that interface.

## I believe in the revolution

The recent essays by Dario Amodei and Mark Zuckerberg interested me precisely because they were not written from outside this transformation.

Amodei does not defend a naive view of artificial intelligence. He describes serious risks: concentration of power, economic disruption, military use, unpredictable model behaviour, and the indirect consequences of accelerating science. He nevertheless believes these risks can be managed and that a substantially better world could emerge on the other side of this transition. [The Adolescence of Technology](https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology)

Zuckerberg focuses more directly on the distribution of power. In his view, a superintelligence concentrated in a few companies or governments would be dangerous. He therefore argues for personal intelligence that is broadly accessible, allowing people to create, learn, build businesses, and pursue their own goals. [The Future Is for Everyone](https://about.fb.com/br/news/2026/07/o-futuro-da-ia-e-para-todos/)

I agree with parts of that reasoning.

Concentrating extraordinary capabilities in the hands of a few actors is a major political problem. There is probably no single superintelligence that could neutrally decide what constitutes a good life for all of humanity. Societies differ, values conflict, and pluralism has to remain real.

I also understand the argument that AI should not be viewed only as a machine for replacing workers. It could become an infrastructure for creation, research, and learning, allowing more people to pursue projects that previously required an entire team or significant resources.

But one essential dimension is missing from these narratives: matter.

Artificial intelligence is not immaterial.

It depends on data centres, processors, memory, networks, cooling systems, buildings, batteries, power lines, and international supply chains. It also depends on minerals, factories, workers, and territories.

Even when a user sees only a chat window, a complete industrial infrastructure is operating behind it.

That is what troubles me about the idea of giving this power to everyone as though equal access were enough to resolve the question of justice. A resource can be accessible at the point of use while remaining deeply unequal in its production.

Everyone may be able to access an AI service, but people do not have the same connectivity, electricity, hardware, ability to pay, or influence over the infrastructure that makes the service possible.

And above all, the planet is not infinite.

## The grid is not ready

The International Energy Agency estimates that data-centre electricity consumption grew by 17% in 2025, while consumption from AI-focused data centres grew even faster. It projects global data-centre electricity use rising from roughly 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030. The agency also points to pressure on grids, transformers, turbines, chips, and supply chains. [IEA, Key Questions on Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

These figures do not represent AI alone. They do show the direction of travel: we are building an industrial demand that is growing faster than the infrastructure's ability to absorb it.

The problem is not simply that the planet produces absolutely too little electricity. The problem is that local grids, generation capacity, connections, permitting processes, and manufacturing supply chains are not sized to absorb this growth at the announced pace.

Data centres are concentrated in particular regions. They arrive with large, rapid, and sometimes highly variable power requirements. They may require new plants, new transmission infrastructure, and investments that local communities do not always control.

AI may genuinely accelerate innovation in energy. It may improve forecasting, optimise grids, or reduce certain forms of waste. But that future promise cannot erase the present cost of building the infrastructure.

## Efficiency does not solve everything

We often hear that models are becoming more efficient. That is true.

The IEA reports that energy use per task can fall sharply through hardware and software improvements. A simple text query may consume relatively little electricity. But that improvement is not sufficient by itself.

Usage is increasing. Models are becoming larger. Video generation, complex reasoning, and autonomous agents consume far more than simple text generation. A lower unit cost can therefore arrive alongside an explosion in total volume.

The technology industry knows this pattern well: making a use cheaper often creates much more of it.

Water raises the same measurement problem. Its impact depends heavily on location, cooling design, climate, and the electricity source. There is therefore no single number that can summarise the water consumed by one query. The United Nations Environment Programme highlights this variation and the difficulty of measuring AI's full lifecycle footprint. [UNEP, Environmental Impact of AI](https://www.unep.org/technical-highlight/how-make-ai-data-centres-more-sustainable)

That does not mean every warning is correct. It means we should stop treating the absence of perfect measurement as evidence that there is no problem.

In his essay, Amodei dismisses concerns about AI water use as insignificant. That may be accurate within some boundaries, regions, or scenarios. But the conclusion feels too quick when applied to a rapidly expanding industry that depends on localised infrastructure and resources that vary dramatically between territories.

## Giving everyone access is not enough

I do not think we should reject artificial intelligence.

I also do not think we should automatically accept every trajectory proposed by the people building it.

The challenge is to move beyond a shallow debate between doom and enthusiasm. It is not enough to ask whether AI is dangerous or beneficial. We need to ask: beneficial for whom, at what cost, with which resources, under what conditions, and under whose control?

A technology does not become just because it is available to everyone. Infrastructure does not become sustainable because it operates behind a digital interface. And a promise of progress is not yet a balance sheet.

I can believe that AI will transform science, education, engineering, and creation while still asking us to count precisely what that transformation consumes. I can recognise the value of distributed intelligence while acknowledging that models, chips, networks, and data centres remain controlled by a very small number of actors.

True democratisation will not only mean opening access to the tools. It will also have to include decisions about infrastructure, resource allocation, acceptable uses, and the distribution of gains.

## A revolution larger than computing

Artificial intelligence will not remain inside the world of computing. It will affect energy, industry, research, medicine, education, defence, work, and probably the way society organises itself.

That is precisely why it is no longer sufficient to think of it only as a software product.

We may be at the beginning of a revolution comparable in scale to the arrival of electricity or personal computing. But revolutions are not only openings of possibility. They redistribute resources, dependencies, and power.

The question is therefore not only how far AI can go.

The question is how much of the world we are willing to mobilise to get there — and who gets to decide what that expenditure means.
