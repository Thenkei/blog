I open a chat window, type a question, and an answer appears. Nothing on screen shows the mines, factories, or power lines behind it.

I work in software and see what AI makes possible: comparing architectures or prototyping an idea in minutes. It changes which ideas are worth trying and makes judgment and verification more important. I believe in that transformation. But when I look at what it takes to run all of this, I am less comfortable.

Two things made me write this: reading two essays, and the reports that keep coming out about data centres and what they consume.

## I believe the promise, despite my reservations

Two recent essays by Dario Amodei and Mark Zuckerberg show the promise the industry sees.

Amodei takes the concentration of power, economic disruption, and model risks seriously. He still argues that these risks can be managed and that this transition can improve the world. [The Adolescence of Technology](https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology)

Zuckerberg argues for widely available personal intelligence rather than superintelligence controlled by a few companies or governments. [The Future Is for Everyone](https://about.fb.com/br/news/2026/07/o-futuro-da-ia-e-para-todos/)

I share their concern about concentrated power. AI can also open up projects in creation, research, and learning that were previously out of reach. But this account often leaves out the physical infrastructure underneath.

“The cloud” hides data centres, processors, cooling, power lines, and supply chains. Follow that chain and you reach minerals, factories, workers, and territories.

A service can be accessible to everyone without the costs of producing it being shared fairly. We do not all have the same connectivity, ability to pay, or influence over its infrastructure. Everyone can use the service, but the data centres still belong to someone and are built somewhere.

## The power socket has joined the roadmap

The International Energy Agency estimates that data-centre electricity consumption grew by 17% in 2025, with consumption from AI-focused data centres growing faster still. It projects global data-centre electricity use rising from roughly 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030, while also pointing to pressure on grids, transformers, turbines, chips, and supply chains. [IEA, Key Questions on Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

Those figures do not measure AI alone. They mostly show how fast demand is growing. Electricity must be generated and delivered in specific places, where local grids may not be ready. Data centres can quickly create heavy demand, along with investments local communities do not always control.

AI may help optimise grids, but that future benefit does not erase the cost of building infrastructure today.

## When “less per task” becomes more in total

The IEA reports that hardware and software can reduce energy use per task. Yet usage is rising, and video generation or agents require more than a simple text query. A single task can use less energy while total consumption goes up.

Water use varies with location, cooling, climate, and electricity source. There is no universal number for “water used by one query”. The United Nations Environment Programme also notes how difficult it is to measure AI's full footprint. [UNEP, Environmental Impact of AI](https://www.unep.org/technical-highlight/how-make-ai-data-centres-more-sustainable)

Amodei sees water concerns as insignificant. That may be true in some contexts. I would find it hard to draw a conclusion that holds everywhere, given how much needs and available resources vary from one place to another.

## Access for everyone, but who pays?

Rather than asking whether AI is good or bad, I would ask: good for whom, at what cost, with whose resources, and under whose control?

I believe AI can transform science, education, and creation. Making it available to everyone takes more than access to a tool. It also depends on where the infrastructure is built, who gets the resources, and who gets the gains.

This revolution redistributes resources, dependencies, and power. How much energy, raw material, and land are we willing to put into it? And who will decide?

I do not have a settled answer yet. I would rather say so.
