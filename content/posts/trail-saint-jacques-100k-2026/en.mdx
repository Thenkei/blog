The 2026 Grand Trail du Saint-Jacques 100K was officially listed at 86 kilometres and 3,500 metres of elevation gain, starting in Monistrol-d’Allier and finishing in Le Puy-en-Velay. [The official course](https://saint-jacques.utmb.world/fr/races/100k) already looked ambitious on paper.

In reality, the race was both harder and easier than I expected.

I genuinely thought I would have no legs left by the end. And yet, after more than fifteen hours of effort, they were still there.

## A start that was far too fast

Just like last year on the 50K, the start from Monistrol-d’Allier immediately sends us uphill. I am running with Blandine, a friend of my partner Charlotte.

Blandine, how should I put this… she started fast. Very fast. Much faster than the average pace we had planned.

As a result, we reach the first aid station thirty-five to forty minutes ahead of our estimate, which was based on a 13:30 race.

At the second aid station, in Saint-Jean-Lachalm, we still have a solid thirty-minute lead over our target schedule. Sometimes we run together, sometimes separately, but everything is going well. The pace is good and our legs are responding.

At that point, I naturally assume the race is going exactly as planned.

## The easy section that was not easy

According to the course profile, the third section should have been a straightforward descent towards Pont-d’Alleyras.

Of course, it was not.

We first pass through a magnificent Natura 2000 protected area. But the peaceful section does not last long: after a very steep descent, we immediately have to climb again. Not a gentle little climb either. It is just as steep, if not steeper.

I was lucky to share this section with another runner. Together, we mostly tried to keep each other’s spirits up.

After about five kilometres, I catch up with Blandine and we finish the section together. But towards the end, we run out of water. I cover the next kilometre and a half, perhaps two, and every metre feels endless.

The volunteers alongside the road tell us:

> “The aid station is 500 metres away.”

Two minutes later, another volunteer tells us the same thing.

Then a spectator says it too:

> “You are nearly there, another 500 metres!”

What can I say? Either we do not share the same mathematics, or we have very different definitions of distance.

The verdict is simple: there were still more than 500 metres to go.

I reach the aid station completely dehydrated. So dehydrated that my strength disappears as soon as I try to leave. I try to restart the machine and follow Blandine, but it is impossible.

## Sixteen kilometres under the sun

The fourth section covers around sixteen kilometres towards Lac du Bouchet. On the profile, it looked fairly straightforward. In reality, it is an endless false flat uphill.

It is the middle of the day, under a blazing sun. This is the hardest part of the race for me.

Several times, I think I can see the trees around Lac du Bouchet.

No. It is just another hill.

A few kilometres later, I finally make out the shape of the lake. This time, it really is there.

During this section, I talk with several runners. I make a few temporary friends, all of us caught up in the same beautiful adventure. Those encounters help enormously: we share the fatigue, the doubts, a few words, and sometimes simply the silence.

As I approach Lac du Bouchet, the feeling in my body changes. The humidity, the coolness of the lake and the surroundings completely reset me.

## The aid station that changed everything

This is the most important aid station of the race for me.

Charlotte is there, along with my parents, my in-laws, Blandine and her family, and several friends. My entire support crew is gathered in one place.

I change my socks, drink, recover a little, and then leave with Blandine for the last major mountain challenge of the day: Mont Devès.

Or so I thought.

The real unexpected difficulty is the mountain before Mont Devès: Mont Recours.

The climb feels endless. It is genuinely difficult. The route goes almost straight up to the summit, with very little respite.

Blandine starts to feel less well, so I continue alone to face my real opponent: Mont Devès.

And then, nothing.

I had built it up so much in my head that the mountain itself turned out to be surprisingly easy. I can even accelerate afterwards on the descending false flat towards Saint-Christophe-sur-Dolaizon.

I am especially happy to keep moving all the way through this section. The previous year, I had completely collapsed after that climb. Even on the descending false flat, I could no longer move properly.

This year is different. The legs are still there. So is the morale.

## The final kilometres

There are about fifteen kilometres left.

I leave the aid station, but my legs start to lock up. There are still two small difficulties, particularly around Eycenac and La Garde.

There is not much climbing left, but I cannot explain why: every little rise starts to hurt.

Night gradually falls. The sunset is beautiful, but my strength is slowly fading.

Fortunately, a fellow runner advises me to put on my headlamp quickly so I do not twist an ankle. I listen.

At the final aid station, at Les Chibottes, there is still one climb, a few false flats going up and down, and then the finish in Le Puy-en-Velay.

And finally, the cathedral.

## A shared finish

The climb to the cathedral is magnificent. I can still run. My legs are still there.

Only a few hundred metres remain. I savour the kilometres completed, the difficult moments, the landscapes, the people I met and every time I wanted to slow down.

Friends are waiting for me at the finish. They run with me all the way to the line.

I jump for joy.

About twenty minutes later, Blandine arrives too.

We achieved our goal: we finished the 100K.

We may not have achieved the time we hoped for at the start. But that is not the point. As the saying goes, it is not the destination that matters; it is the journey that makes it worthwhile.

## Thank you

I want to thank my entire support crew. Without them, I probably would not have made it: Charlotte, my parents, my in-laws, Blandine’s family and all my friends who were there along the course or at the finish.

Thank you as well to everyone who came to support us, to the volunteers, the spectators and all the runners we met on the trails.

And of course, thank you to the entire Trail du Saint-Jacques organisation for an incredible event.
