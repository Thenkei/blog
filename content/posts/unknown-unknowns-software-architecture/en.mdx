## The diagram stops at production

Architecture reviews handle known risks well: load, availability, migrations, retries. They can cover all four and leave the most important assumption in the margins.

An assumption may remain implicit, or a failure mode may be absent from the diagram. These are the **unknown unknowns**: risks that never made it onto the list.

Thariq Shihipar's [A Field Guide to Fable: Finding Your Unknowns](https://x.com/trq212/article/2073100352921215386) describes the gap between the *map* supplied to an agent and the real *territory*. In software, too, no document fully represents operators, historical data, and production usage.

## Production always brings a plus-one

A design can pass every written scenario and still fail when a request is retried during the first attempt, or an event arrives after its target is deleted.

The tests did not lie; we asked the questions we planned for. The danger is confusing “all tests passed” with “we modelled everything”.

## Three comfortable stories

**The exhaustive diagram.** More boxes do little to represent time, concurrency, or manual intervention.

**The checklist as proof.** It records known risks, not assumptions missing from everyone's model.

**The omniscient agent.** It can inspect more sources, but only finds what left a trace in them.

None can certify that a review is complete.

## Ask questions with teeth

A review of the nominal path describes the components, but what happens if a call is duplicated, reordered, or replayed by an operator?

“Will it scale?” invites a generic answer. “Which cardinality assumption breaks this component first?” sends the reviewer looking for evidence.

Before an important decision, our internal tool combines **gates** for known invariants, a **find blind spots** pass for unproven assumptions, and **adversarial reviews** across operations, security, data, and deployment.

The tool connects each question to evidence, or makes its absence visible:

- Which financial or domain invariant could fail silently?
- Which dependency shares our blast radius?
- Which manual operation bypasses the nominal path?
- Which signal would expose the issue before a customer does?
- Which recovery path remains possible when the assumption is false?

A human reviewer remains accountable: a known risk can be accepted, an invisible one cannot.

## Build the fire exits while it is still quiet

We will not predict every failure. We can make surprises visible, contain their impact, and recover:

1. **Reduce** assumptions through explicit contracts and executable invariants.
2. **Isolate** components and permissions to contain the blast radius.
3. **Detect** divergence through metrics, traces, reconciliation, and domain alerts.
4. **Recover** through idempotency, controlled replay, kill switches, rollback, or compensation.

One final question: if the assumption becomes false tomorrow, will we notice and respond?

## The map stays unfinished

This method can create noise and delay decisions that are easy to undo. Scale the effort to the potential impact: a financial calculation or irreversible migration needs more evidence than an isolated experiment.

Reviews also inherit the blind spots of their sources. Incidents, operator feedback, and production evidence remain irreplaceable. The goal is uncertainty we can observe and own.
