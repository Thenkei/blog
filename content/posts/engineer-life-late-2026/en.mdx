I built a video factory to promote our releases, with proper communication around what we deliver. It has a repository, conventions, and scripts. So far, familiar territory. Then I open a storyboard, work on the narration, and find myself discussing the pacing of a scene.

Staff engineer, with a side specialisation in video editing. I had not planned that one.

I also do data engineering, BI, analysis, some SRE-ish work, and prepare designs. These activities already overlap considerably with engineering. With agents, I can take on more of them and start initiatives beyond my role's usual scope.

The terminal has become a place from which I prepare much of this work, from a data pipeline to a storyboard. The interface changes little; the questions I need to answer change considerably.

## Staff engineering is already a cross-functional role

Reducing my role to reviewing PRs and checking a design does not describe what I do. It also involves framing the problem, making tradeoffs, handling dependencies between teams, and following the work through delivery. Project tracking belongs in that scope. I did not become a project manager the day an agent learned to generate a schedule.

The boundaries were already porous. A technical decision requires understanding the intended outcome, who will use the result, and what the teams can maintain. Those discussions have long extended beyond the code of the service we are working on.

What changes is how much work I can undertake myself around those decisions, and the fields in which I can try something.

## Data engineering, BI, and analysis are different kinds of work

I actually do these activities. Grouping them under “pulling a number from the warehouse” leaves out much of the work.

In **data engineering**, we need to build pipelines and understand what they carry: grain, duplicates, freshness, and recovery after failure. A running pipeline does not guarantee that the data it produces is fit for its intended use.

In **BI**, the work also involves defining metrics and making them useful. Which population is counted? As of which date? Which filters change the result? A polished dashboard can present a metric whose definition nobody agrees on.

In **data analysis**, we need to start from a question, choose what to compare, examine differences, and determine what the data supports. Writing the query does not settle those questions.

Agents can help prepare SQL, explore pipeline code, or propose a visualisation. We still need to understand the data and its limits to judge the result. Moving into another field means learning what matters in that field.

## SRE-ish work and preparing designs

I am keeping the “ish”: opening a production dashboard does not give me an entire specialisation. But reliability, observability, diagnosis, and operational conditions take up a real part of my work. The question extends beyond “does the code work?”: how will we know when it stops working, and how will we intervene?

On the **design** side, I prepare proposals. That means thinking about the user journey, the information presented, and interface states: loading, no results, errors, actions that are available or forbidden.

Being able to prepare a design also makes discussion more concrete. We can examine a flow and its choices with the people involved, then revise it. That calls for different criteria from a code review.

## The video factory extends beyond that scope

This is the clearest example of the change for me. I set up a tool to produce videos and promote releases. Building it involves engineering, followed by the communication work we prepare with it.

The factory organises the brief, storyboard, scenes, narration, and sound. It lets us work on those elements together. We need to choose what to say, who it is for, what to show, and what deserves to stay in the video.

The changelog provides useful information. Communicating a release also means explaining what it brings to the people who will use it and preparing a presentation that speaks to them. That is editorial work, with decisions about content and form.

Agents make this initiative more accessible from my usual working environment. I can prepare narration, build scenes, and iterate on the result. We still need to watch the video, listen to what it says, and decide whether it accurately presents what we delivered. A successful export cannot answer those questions. Unfortunately, good taste still has no exit code.

## More possibilities, more skills to learn

Context switching remains an issue. Moving from a metric definition to a design, then to narration, requires changing criteria. Staying in the same terminal does not remove that effort.

But switching tasks is not the only thing I take from this. I can also undertake things I would not necessarily have started before. The video factory exists because I could work on both the tool and what we wanted to do with it.

I like that porosity. It gives me reasons to learn, prepare proposals, and work with people whose expertise differs from mine. It also requires knowing when to involve someone who understands the subject better.

My job title has not changed. I have still added a video factory to my projects. I may need a slightly longer line on the CV.
