My terminal has acquired a few responsibilities lately. Git, tests, deploys: familiar territory. Release announcements, milestone plans, communication drafts: less familiar. All of it now fits behind the same blinking cursor.

We were promised the end of the keyboard. I seem to have found more reasons to use mine.

## Same terminal, longer job description

The odd part is how little changes on screen. I ask an agent to investigate a race condition, then ask it to make a release announcement less embarrassing. Both requests look roughly the same. They require very different answers.

The terminal was already where I spent much of my working day. Agents made it useful for work I used to do elsewhere, or leave to someone else. I can stay in the same window to prepare code, a changelog, and a communication plan. Whether I should own all three is a separate question, one the prompt doesn't ask.

At least the progress bar is consistent. It moves, I relax. It stalls, I check whether I've broken something. Sometimes it's just waiting for me to approve a command. Oh, wait, there's auto mode for that too. Apparently even my click on "approve" needed automating.

## Eight jobs, one chair

Beyond shipping code, I now help communicate the releases: what we're starting, what the first milestone delivers, what people can actually use when it goes live. The work starts well before the final announcement.

An agent can help prepare all of that. Which is convenient, because the original job hasn't got any smaller:

- **Staff engineer.** Check the design, review the code, think through the failure modes. Still the job on the résumé.
- **Improvised marketer.** Explain why anyone should care about the release. A technically accurate changelog can still give readers no reason to open it.
- **Project manager.** Keep the milestones and risks current. Generating a plan is quick. Finding out that a dependency has slipped takes a conversation.
- **Data analyst on demand.** Pull numbers from the warehouse for "one quick question." Three joins later, I'm explaining why the two dashboards disagree.
- **Community manager for the release.** Read the feedback and answer questions. Occasionally discover that the sentence everyone misunderstood is the one I thought was clearest.
- **Designer between builds.** Move a button eight pixels, ask the agent for three variants, then pick the first one. The build is still running.
- **Technical writer.** Update the README to explain how to run the project. Follow my own instructions and discover half the environment variables are missing. At least I've found my reader.
- **Release video editor.** Prepare a thirty-second demo. Spend twenty minutes cutting the three seconds where I'm looking for the right tab.

At this point, I do a bit of everything, including things I have no business spending this much time on. My job title still fits on one line; my day doesn't. That doesn't make me eight specialists. I'm still a conscientious engineer: I check the numbers, test what I ship, and ask for a review when I'm outside my expertise. Even if I spent the previous ten minutes choosing the demo thumbnail.

I can prepare more of this myself than I used to. But eight drafts ready before lunch are still eight drafts to review. And lunch is still lunch. I'd like to keep that feature.

## The expensive part is switching

There is no visible boundary between those tasks. A production bug and an announcement edit arrive in the same conversation, sometimes with equally confident suggestions from the agent.

That makes it easy to spend twenty minutes polishing a hook while a design decision waits. The announcement feels nearly done; the design needs another uncomfortable conversation. Guess which one is more tempting.

Reviewing the work also means changing standards. For code, I look at behavior, tests, permissions, failure paths. For an announcement, I need to check what is available, who can use it, and whether the wording promises more than we delivered. Passing tests won't answer those questions.

The tool makes switching cheap enough that I stop noticing how often I do it. I notice later, when the afternoon is gone and the difficult task is still open.

## Before I hit send

I don't need a new framework for this. I need to leave time for review and bring in the people who know what I don't.

Before merging code, check the diff and the evidence. Before publishing an announcement, check its claims against the feature people can use. Before committing to a milestone, check the dependencies and capacity with the team. An agent can prepare the material for each decision. It can't make the team available next Tuesday.

The same person doesn't have to approve everything. If I've drafted a message about a feature, someone who will use that feature may catch a misleading sentence much faster than I will. Owning the draft doesn't make me the best final reviewer.

I like being able to do more from the terminal. I like it considerably less when that turns into an expectation that I'll own every task it can produce.

For now, I'm keeping the extra capabilities. And I'm trying to close a task before opening the next one. The terminal can multitask. My attention remains stubbornly single-threaded. <small><em>Almost. I'm a robot too, after all.</em></small>
