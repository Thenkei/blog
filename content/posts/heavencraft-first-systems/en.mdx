I did not start coding with a career plan, a particularly noble passion for computing, or the ambition to become an engineer.

I was probably fourteen or fifteen — I no longer remember exactly — and my first objective was to create bots for Dofus. Nothing especially admirable, and certainly not the part of my journey that I would turn into a tutorial today.

But that is where it began.

## Starting without a plan

After those first attempts, I built all sorts of small personal projects. Some were useful. Others were considerably less so.

I made a few slightly silly applications to say "I love you" to people who have since become ex-partners. I also started several small games with friends and tinkered with anything that gave me a reason to learn something new.

None of those projects was supposed to become a product. They mainly let me discover the very tangible pleasure of starting from nothing and making something work.

Then HeavenCraft arrived.

## When HeavenCraft changed the scale

In high school, I had the opportunity to join HeavenCraft, a French Minecraft server with a substantial community.

I am quoting these orders of magnitude from memory: the figure I remember is more than 35,000 players welcomed in total. Some weeks saw more than 1,000 active players, and roughly 100 to 200 people could be connected at the same time.

For a high-school student who had mostly built for himself or a few friends, the change was immense. My code was no longer a local experiment. Real people used it, worked around it, misunderstood it, or made it their own.

## Games, worlds, and an economy

I spent an enormous amount of time on the project. I developed many systems required to operate the server, then worked with a small team to add several games.

We automated *Dé à Coudre*, a French drop-and-build minigame familiar to Minecraft players. We also built Battle Royale games before Fortnite made the format ubiquitous, as well as PvP maps closer to the Call of Duty multiplayer experience, with four-versus-four, six-versus-six, or eight-versus-eight matches.

HeavenCraft was not merely a collection of minigames. We operated several worlds and built a complete system for buying land or plots through the server's economy.

Looking back, I can better see the variety of problems this represented. Rules, uses, spaces, and economic mechanisms all had to coexist inside an environment that was already alive.

## What it taught me

At the time, I did not yet use words such as product, platform, or operations. Yet I was already encountering some of those questions.

A feature does not exist in isolation. It changes a shared environment. Players need to understand it, it must work with everything around it, and it must withstand uses that its author cannot fully control.

It was probably the most formative project of my high-school years. HeavenCraft taught me that writing code is one thing; building something that other people take ownership of is another.

## After HeavenCraft

I went on to start many small projects with friends, often around video games. Some moved forward; others quickly faded. None reached as many hands.

That does not make them less valuable, but HeavenCraft remains separate in my mind.

It was probably not the most elegant code I have ever written. It was certainly not the most structured project. It was, however, the first time something I built truly moved beyond my own computer and became a space used by a community.

Long before my first engineering job, that was where I understood why I loved building systems.
