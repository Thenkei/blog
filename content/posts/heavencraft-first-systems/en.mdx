I did not start coding with a career plan or the ambition to become an engineer.

I was probably fourteen or fifteen, I no longer remember exactly, and my first goal was to write bots for Dofus. Nothing to be proud of, and certainly not something I would turn into a tutorial today.

But that is where it began.

## Starting without a plan

After those first attempts, I built all sorts of small personal projects. Some were useful. Others much less.

I made a few slightly silly applications to say "I love you" to people who have since become exes. I also started several small games with friends and tinkered with anything that gave me a reason to learn something new.

None of those projects was meant to become a product. They mostly let me discover the pleasure of building something that works.

Then HeavenCraft arrived.

## Joining HeavenCraft

In high school, I had the opportunity to join HeavenCraft, a French Minecraft server with a large community.

I am quoting these orders of magnitude from memory: the figure I remember is more than 35,000 players welcomed in total. Some weeks saw more than 1,000 active players, and roughly 100 to 200 people could be connected at the same time.

For a high-school student who had mostly built for himself or a few friends, it was a huge change. Real people used my code, worked around it, misunderstood it, or made it their own.

## Games, worlds, and an economy

I spent an enormous amount of time on the project. I developed many of the systems the server needed to run, then added several games with a small team.

We automated *Dé à Coudre*, a French drop-and-build minigame familiar to Minecraft players. We also built Battle Royale games before Fortnite made the format ubiquitous, as well as PvP maps closer to the Call of Duty multiplayer experience, with four-versus-four, six-versus-six, or eight-versus-eight matches.

There was more than minigames. We ran several worlds and had a whole system for buying land or plots through the server's economy.

Looking back, I can better see the variety of problems this represented. Every addition had to work with the worlds, the games, and the economy that players were already using.

## What it taught me

At the time, I did not use words like product, platform, or operations. But I was already running into some of those questions.

A new feature had to be understood by players, work with the rest of the server, and hold up against uses I had not planned for.

What players did most was extend the core mechanics: they found possibilities in them that I had not planned.

It is probably the project I learned the most from in high school. I had written code before, but never something that other people made their own.

## After HeavenCraft

I went on to start many small projects with friends, often around video games. Some moved forward, others quickly faded. None reached as many people.

I do not think less of them, but HeavenCraft remains apart for me.

The code was probably not very elegant, and the project not very structured. But for the first time, thousands of people were using what I was building.

That is where I understood why I like building systems, long before my first engineering job.
