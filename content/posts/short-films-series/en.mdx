For the article [What I Call a Software Factory](../posts/software-factory-loops), I made a 29-second animated film. An engineer answers his agents' questions until he disappears under the bubbles, then loops run without him and he only stamps three decisions.

I liked the format, so I am making a series. Each episode takes an article already published on this blog and tells it in twenty to forty-five seconds. It is published in a very short article that links back to the original one.

## What stays the same from one episode to the next

- **The characters.** The same engineer and the same cream robots, dark visors and amber eyes, as in the Software Factory film.
- **The set.** The night workshop of the blog's cover: navy and teal shadows, warm lamp light.
- **The sound.** Music, sound effects, and a few short lines from the engineer, in my cloned voice. Each film also reads with the sound off, and the lines have captions.
- **The words.** The text on screen comes from the original article, and so do the numbers. The humour is in the pictures and the timing, not in the text.
- **The ending.** The last three seconds give the series name, one sentence from the article, and the original article with its year.

## How the films are made

Each film is a folder in [the video factory](../posts/video-factory-remotion-agents): a brief, a storyboard, the on-screen text and the scenes.

The pictures are painted by an image generation model, driven through the Codex command line. There are sets, and drawings of characters and objects on a green background that are keyed out afterwards. The character sheet and the blog's cover are references for every image, so the engineer looks like the same engineer from one film to the next.

Remotion animates these pictures: camera moves on the sets, drawings swapped on the beat, small objects moving across. The words on screen are set by Remotion, never by the image model. The music is generated by a script, locally, on a tempo grid that the edit also follows. The sound effects come from ElevenLabs' sound effects model, from a short description, and each one is placed on the frame where its event happens.

Before a film is delivered, the factory checks the loudness, reads each line of text on its frame with OCR, and looks for blank frames and one-frame jumps.
