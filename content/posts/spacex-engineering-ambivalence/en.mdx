My relationship with Elon Musk and SpaceX can be summed up by a contradiction: I can be deeply impressed by what an organisation manages to build without subscribing to the entire vision surrounding that work.

That distinction matters. Technical admiration should never require buying the rest of the ideological package.

## Before the subject became unavoidable

I was already following companies associated with Elon Musk around 2013 or 2014, before the subject took up so much media space around me.

I deliberately say companies associated with him. They were not all created by him alone, and reducing the work of entire organisations to one person would be false. What interested me most was the capacity to found, join, fund, or transform exceptionally ambitious technology projects.

Unfortunately for me, I never invested in the stock market. That early fascination had no noticeable effect on my bank account.

## What amazes me about SpaceX

What strikes me about SpaceX is not only the spectacle of a launch. It is the integration of very different disciplines around one system: propulsion, mechanics, software, materials, operations, and safety.

Watching a launch vehicle lift off or return to land makes a small part of that work visible. Behind those images are teams, tests, failures, corrections, and a standard of execution that is difficult to imagine from the outside.

That engineering is what amazes me, not the story of a solitary genius. A rocket is not built through the will of one executive. It requires a collective effort that is precise and sustained.

## Where I diverge

I do not share the idea that humanity's future must necessarily be played out far from Earth.

Exploring space, producing knowledge, and pushing certain technological limits all seem essential to me. Treating another planet as a fallback or a new civilisational project is far less convincing.

To me, our place remains here. Earth is not a temporary stop that we can neglect while waiting for something better. It is the environment that makes our existence possible, and no achievement in space should become an excuse to forget that responsibility.

## Orbit as a responsibility

Another concern sits alongside my admiration: the possibility that Earth orbit could become a graveyard of satellites and objects that are no longer usable.

I am not saying that outcome is inevitable. I am saying that deployment speed must be matched by equally serious responsibility for end of life, tracking, collision risks, and the governance of this shared environment.

This is not an abstract concern: [the European Space Agency's 2026 Space Environment Report](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/publications/Space_Environment_Report_I10R0_20260501.pdf) specifically tracks orbital congestion, fragmentation events, and compliance with debris-mitigation measures.

A significant part of today's technological world depends on space-based systems. Permanently degrading their environment would turn industrial success into systemic risk.

## Holding both ideas at once

I can admire SpaceX and remain critical. I can be fascinated by Elon Musk's entrepreneurial ambition without making him a moral or political model. I can want more space exploration while believing that Earth remains our priority.

This contradiction does not need to be resolved. It needs to be examined honestly.

Perhaps that is also part of loving engineering: refusing to confuse the power of what we can build with the wisdom of every decision about how to use it.
