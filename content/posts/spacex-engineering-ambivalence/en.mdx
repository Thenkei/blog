SpaceX reliably produces two reactions in me.

The first arrives at liftoff: this is extraordinary engineering. The second follows close behind: I am much less convinced by the vision wrapped around it.

It is tempting to resolve that contradiction by picking a team. Either applaud the whole project or reject the whole thing. I would rather keep the contradiction. Technical admiration should not come bundled with an ideological subscription.

## T–13 years: good instinct, zero shares

I was already following companies associated with Elon Musk around 2013 or 2014, before the subject occupied quite so much of the media landscape around me.

I choose the phrase “associated with” deliberately. He did not create all of those companies alone, and attributing the work of entire organisations to one person would be false. What caught my attention was his ability to found, join, fund, or transform technology projects with almost unreasonable ambition.

Unfortunately, I never invested in the stock market. My early fascination did absolutely nothing for my bank balance.

## A rocket is a group project

The launch is the visible part. It is also the shortest explanation of what impresses me.

SpaceX brings propulsion, mechanics, software, materials, operations, and safety into one system that has to work outside a slide deck. A vehicle lifting off is spectacular. A vehicle returning to land is almost absurdly so. Behind both are teams, tests, failures, corrections, and a standard of execution that is hard to imagine from the outside.

That collective engineering deserves the applause.

The solitary-genius story does not. A rocket is not built by the force of one executive's personality. It is built by people doing precise, sustained work across disciplines, including the unglamorous work that never appears in the launch footage.

## Mars is not an exit plan

This is where my admiration and the surrounding vision part company.

I want us to explore space, produce knowledge, and push technological limits. None of that requires treating another planet as humanity's fallback or its inevitable civilisational project.

Our place, to me, remains here. Earth is not a waiting room we can leave untidy because a more exciting destination appears on the departures board. It is the environment that makes our existence possible.

An achievement in space can enlarge our understanding of Earth. It should not become an excuse to neglect it.

## Orbit is not the attic

There is another tension closer to home: the risk of turning Earth orbit into a graveyard of satellites and unusable objects.

I am not claiming this outcome is inevitable. I am saying that deployment speed needs an equally serious plan for end of life, tracking, collision risk, and governance of a shared environment.

This concern is concrete enough to measure. [The European Space Agency's 2026 Space Environment Report](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/publications/Space_Environment_Report_I10R0_20260501.pdf) tracks orbital congestion, fragmentation events, and compliance with debris-mitigation measures.

Much of today's technological world depends on space-based systems. If industrial success permanently degrades the environment those systems need, the victory has quietly turned into systemic risk.

## Ambivalence survives re-entry

I can admire SpaceX and remain critical. I can find Elon Musk's entrepreneurial ambition fascinating without treating him as a moral or political model. I can want more space exploration while keeping Earth as the priority.

Those positions do not cancel one another. Together, they are more honest than the demand to become either a fan or an opponent.

Perhaps this is part of loving engineering: being moved by the power of what people can build, then staying long enough to ask whether every proposed use of that power is wise.
