SpaceX gives me two reactions, almost at the same time.

At liftoff, I think: what a feat of engineering. Right after, I wonder: does that mean I have to buy into the whole vision that comes with it?

I could settle it by picking a side: applaud everything or reject everything. I would rather keep both. I can admire the engineering without signing up for the ideology.

## I followed early and never invested

I was already following companies associated with Elon Musk around 2013 or 2014, before they got so much media coverage.

I say “associated with” on purpose. He did not create all of those companies alone, and crediting one person with the work of entire organisations would be wrong. What caught my attention was how he founded, joined, funded, or transformed technology projects with almost unreasonable ambition.

Unfortunately, I never invested in the stock market. Following them early did absolutely nothing for my bank account.

## What impresses me

The launch is the visible part. I do not have one launch in mind: I followed them over the years, and no single one stands out.

SpaceX brings propulsion, mechanics, software, materials, operations, and safety into one system, and that system has to work somewhere other than in a presentation. Watching a launcher lift off is spectacular. Watching it come back and land fascinates me even more. Behind those images are teams, tests, failures, corrections, and a standard of execution that is hard to imagine from the outside.

That collective engineering is what deserves the applause.

I do not believe in the lone-genius story. A rocket is built by people doing precise work over a long time, across several disciplines, including all the unglamorous tasks that never appear in the launch broadcast.

## I do not believe in Mars as a plan B

This is where I stop following.

I want us to explore space, produce knowledge, and push some technological limits. None of that requires treating another planet as humanity's fallback or its inevitable civilisational project.

For me, our place is here. Earth is the environment that makes our existence possible, and a more exciting destination is no reason to take less care of it.

Space missions can even help us understand Earth better.

## Orbital debris

My other concern is much closer to us: Earth orbit could fill up with dead satellites and unusable objects.

I do not think this is inevitable. But deploying this fast calls for an equally serious plan for end of life, tracking, collision risk, and governance of this shared space.

It is also something we can measure. [The European Space Agency's 2026 Space Environment Report](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/publications/Space_Environment_Report_I10R0_20260501.pdf) tracks orbital congestion, fragmentation events, and compliance with debris-mitigation measures.

Much of today's technological world depends on space-based systems. If industrial success permanently degrades the environment those systems need, we create a risk for everyone who depends on them.

## Admiring and staying critical

I admire SpaceX and I remain critical of it. Elon Musk's entrepreneurial ambition fascinates me, but I do not see him as a moral or political model. And I want more space exploration, as long as Earth stays the priority.

I do not see a contradiction there. It seems more honest to me than having to be either a fan or an opponent.

I am still impressed by what these teams build. That does not stop me from asking what we choose to do with it.
